Every radio firmware on Earth is written in C. The chip vendors ship C. The examples are C. The forum posts from
2011 that you end up reading at midnight are C. When you've got 96 KB of RAM and no operating system, C is what you
reach for without thinking.

So naturally, I wrote Ferrite in Rust.

Before you close the tab: this isn't a "rewrite it in Rust" sermon. I picked it before a single line of Ferrite
ran on the radio, for boring, practical reasons. They just happen to be very good boring, practical reasons.

## It's a computer. With a transmitter.

Pop the back off a Radtel RT-950 Pro and you'll find an Artery AT32F403A (a Cortex-M4 rated for 240 MHz; Ferrite
runs it at up to 120), 96 KB of RAM, two Beken BK4829 transceiver chips and an SI4732 for broadcast and shortwave.
That little M4 decides what frequency the transmitter keys up on, how hard, and for how long.

Most software bugs give you a crash dialog. Radio firmware bugs give you a carrier on the wrong frequency, and
possibly a polite email from someone with a direction-finding antenna.

So I wanted the meanest compiler I could find. Rust's will flat out refuse to build a whole family of classic
firmware bugs: memory used after it's gone, two tasks scribbling on the same thing at once, the `match` that forgot
a case. Every one of those is a bug I never had to hunt down with a radio in one hand and a soldering iron in the
other.

## The bench is where time goes to die

Here's the loop for radio firmware: build an image, flash it through the stock bootloader over the programming
cable (about 85 seconds of staring at a progress bar), then go test it on a real radio with a real signal. Minutes,
every single time.

A bug the compiler catches costs seconds. A bug the bench catches costs a coffee. A bug the bench *doesn't* catch
costs a lot more than that.

I also build Ferrite with AI tools helping, which makes a strict compiler worth its weight in gold. It's the
grumpiest code reviewer I've ever had, and it reviews every line, mine or not. If it compiles, a lot of things are
already true.

## Trust, but measure

I didn't take Rust on faith. My first test image for the radio was C, the usual way. Then I ported it to Rust,
feature for feature, same screens, same console, and put them side by side.

The Rust build came out about a third bigger. Ouch? A little. The radio has the room, and I'll trade bytes for
bugs-that-can't-happen any day.

But size does bite. The AT32's flash has a fast part (the first 256 KB, zero wait states) and a slow part, and
anything that runs often had better live in the fast part. Rust happily copies a generic function into every place
that calls it, which is great until it isn't: one innocent extra branch in a drawing routine once cost us 18 KB in
a single change. Eighteen kilobytes. For an if-statement. We check the size map on every change to shared code now.
Lesson learned, the expensive way.

## Same code, two homes

Here's the part I didn't see coming. Everything above the registers is plain `no_std` Rust that builds just as
happily for a PC as for the radio: the screens, the menus, the APRS decoder, the channel storage, the link to
Ferrite Console. That turns out to be a superpower:

- **Thousands of tests** run on a computer on every change, before anything gets near the radio. (Testing gets
  its own post. There's a lot to say.)
- **The screen recordings on this site** are the firmware's own drawing code, running on a laptop.
- **Ferrite Console** runs the radio's own code, compiled for the browser, to read and write your channels. The
  two can't disagree about what a channel looks like, because they're literally the same code.

## Nothing weird mid key-up

Ferrite never allocates memory while it runs. No heap. Everything gets its spot when the image is built, so there's
no "out of memory" halfway through a transmission. The `unsafe` code that pokes hardware registers directly lives
in one small, fenced-off corner, and every block of it has to explain itself.

And if something does go sideways? A panic compiles down to a single trap instruction. The core faults, the fault
handler slams everything into a safe state with the transmitter off, and the radio stops. No limping along with a
carrier up, hoping for the best.

## Naps are free

Ferrite runs on [Embassy](https://embassy.dev), an async framework built for exactly this kind of chip. The radio,
the screen, the keys, APRS and GPS are each a task that naps until there's work to do. And when everything's
napping, Ferrite drops the core from 120 MHz all the way down to 24.

On the bench, sitting on a quiet channel: 18.6 mA, down from 64.7 mA with the clock pinned at full speed. That's
about 107 hours on the stock battery pack. My exact words when the number came in were "this is massive", and I'm
sticking with them. (Fair warning: resting is the best case. Scanning, mashing keys and APRS bring the clock back
up while they run.)
